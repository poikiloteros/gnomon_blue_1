Stylos
The name

A stylus is a plain tool. One end cuts the letter into the wax; turn it over and the flat end wipes it away. Write, read, change your mind, write it again — that is most of the job.

Saepe stilum uertas, iterum quae digna legi sint scripturus.

Turn the stylus often, if you mean to write something worth a second reading.

Horace, Sermones 1.10.72–73
The work

Structure first. Then the surface.

We make websites for people whose material is complicated — a collection, an archive, a research programme, a body of work built up over years.

That means settling the structure before anything is styled: what the thing is, who has to read it, and how it is meant to last. The design follows from that, and is the better for it.

And then it is handed over properly. Whatever ought to be yours to change ends up yours to change — including how the thing looks. The two inks in the corner of this page are not a demonstration: and the whole site turns, drawing and all. Every commission leaves with controls of its own.

Method

How a commission runs.

I

Read

It starts with your material: the sources, the audiences, and what the institution or the stage actually requires.

II

Structure

Architecture before decoration. Data models, provenance and navigation that will still make sense in ten years.

III

Draw

Typography, colour, pace and atmosphere, set to the register of the work — scholarly, civic or theatrical.

IV

Keep

Documentation, handover, hosting advice and quiet maintenance. Sites built to be kept, not rebuilt.

Studio

A practice rooted in classics, built for the present.

Stylos is a one-person practice led by Arkadiy Avdokhin, a classicist and archaeologist at the University of Oxford and Wolfson College — a doctorate in Classics from King’s College London, a Marie Skłodowska-Curie Fellowship at the Oxford Faculty of Classics, and current work towards a digital 3D and visualisation lab with the Ashmolean Museum.

The design work grew out of the research: a public platform for the inscriptions and 3D-modelled structures of late antique Aphrodisias, and a scholarly database for a Vienna-based project on Roman-era papyri. The same habits — structure, provenance, longevity, multilingual and accessible by default — shape every site, whether it serves a grant or a stage.

Engagements are small and scoped — comfortable with grant timelines and work-package deliverables, and corresponding by email.
